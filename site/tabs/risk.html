A transparent, public-data emulation of CrossBorder Capital's forex risk index — a composite of risk & liquidity conditions that, advanced by ~12 months, runs inverse to the trade-weighted US dollar. High risk / tight liquidity today → a weaker dollar a year later; low risk / abundant liquidity → a firmer dollar.

Methodology & honest caveats

How this is built — and where it differs from the original

What CrossBorder's forex risk index measures

By CrossBorder's own description, the index tracks the balance between private-sector liquidity (strong economy, high return on capital — dollar-bullish) and central-bank / Fed liquidity (dollar-bearish). Michael Howell cites implied equity volatility, the yield curve / term premia, and credit spreads as his risk-appetite proxies. The mechanism is flow-based: risk and liquidity conditions today shape cross-border capital flows, which move the dollar with a lag of roughly a year.

The five components (all public, all oriented so high = more forex risk)

  • creditMoody's Baa−Aaa corporate quality spread — the anchor; wide = stress. FRED: BAA, AAA
  • volatilityImplied equity vol, VXO spliced to VIX from 1990. FRED: VXOCLS, VIXCLS
  • equityNegative 12-month S&P 500 momentum (falling equities = risk-off). Yahoo: ^GSPC
  • commodityNegative 12-month oil momentum (a global reflation/cycle proxy). FRED: WTISPLC
  • real rateReal fed funds (fed funds − CPI YoY) — policy tightness / liquidity stance. FRED: FEDFUNDS, CPIAUCSL

Each is z-scored over the full sample, equal-weighted into a composite, and mapped to 0–100 via the normal CDF. The construction is fully exogenous to the dollar — no exchange-rate term appears anywhere, so the lead can't be mechanical. The yield curve was tested and dropped: over this sample it carried the wrong sign and no incremental signal for the dollar.

The dollar series

The Fed's nominal major-currencies trade-weighted index (TWEXMMTH, 1980–2019, matching Howell's chart range) chain-linked to its successor Advanced Foreign Economies index (TWEXAFEGSMTH) to run through 2026.

The R² gap is the honest headline. This emulation reproduces the sign, shape and ~12-month lead of Howell's relationship, but reaches R²≈0.12 versus his published 0.34. The difference is almost entirely his proprietary capital-flow and liquidity data: an optimal in-sample blend of every public risk/liquidity series I tested tops out near R²≈0.19. Public market data captures the risk-cycle skeleton; it can't see the flows.

What holds up, and what to distrust

  • Sign stability: the core credit-spread lead is negative in every decade (−0.44 / −0.25 / −0.34 / −0.41 across the 80s–90s, 2000s, 2010s, 2020s), so the relationship is not a single-era artefact.
  • Genuinely leading: the two-sided cross-correlation against the dollar's YoY change is ≈0 coincident and ≈0 on the index-lagging side, and only turns materially negative once the index leads — reaching −0.34 by 12 months and plateauing through ~22 months. So this is a real lead, not a coincident echo, and 12 months sits right where the relationship matures.
  • Compare to the change, not the level: the advanced index correlates −0.34 with the dollar's YoY change but +0.23 with the raw level — wrong sign, contaminated by the dollar's secular trend. A rate-of-change indicator must be read against a rate of change; the scatter and time-series here both do that.
  • Lookahead: z-scores use the full sample (as Howell's in-sample chart implicitly does). A strict point-in-time version would standardise on an expanding window; treat the historical scatter as descriptive, not a live backtest.
  • Excluded despite a strong in-sample fit: C&I loan growth looked powerful (−0.35) but flipped hard positive in 2020–25 on the PPP surge — a good reminder that in-sample strength ≠ robustness.

Bottom line: a faithful skeleton of the CrossBorder concept, useful for reading the direction and cycle position of the risk/liquidity backdrop, with the flow layer that gives Howell his extra explanatory power openly acknowledged as missing.
